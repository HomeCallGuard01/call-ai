Talking to an Elderly Parent About Scam Calls

The subject matters more than most family conversations about ageing — and it's easy to get the tone wrong. Here's what tends to work.

Bringing up scam calls with a parent can feel loaded — it can land as "I think you're losing the ability to look after yourself," even when that's nowhere near what's meant. A few things reliably help.

Lead with your own worry, not their vulnerability

"I keep reading about how convincing these scam calls have got, and it would put my mind at rest if we sorted a few things out together" lands very differently to "you need to be careful, you could get scammed." The first is about your own peace of mind; the second implies a judgement about their capability.

Use a real example, not a hypothetical

Scam calls are common enough that a specific recent news story or a call you've had yourself is easy to find, and far more persuasive than a general warning. "Did you see that story about the courier fraud scam going round — I had no idea it worked like that" opens a conversation naturally, rather than starting from "I'm worried about you."

Make it a two-way conversation, not a briefing

Ask what calls they've been getting, rather than only telling them what to watch for. Many people have already noticed an uptick in suspicious calls and are glad to talk about it — the conversation often goes more easily than expected once it's clear you're not there to tell them off.

Agree a rule together, rather than imposing one

The most effective protection is a simple agreement made calmly, in advance: no money, card details, passwords or one-time codes are ever given over the phone, and no one is let into the house, without hanging up first and checking with a trusted family member or the organisation directly. Framing this as a shared family rule — one you'd apply to yourself too — makes it much easier to accept than a rule aimed only at them.

Separate "getting help" from "losing independence"

Where practical steps like call-blocking or a monitoring service come up, it's worth being explicit that trusted family and friends are never affected — their calls always get through exactly as before. The change only applies to calls from people they don't already know, which helps it feel like a targeted precaution rather than a loss of control over their own phone.

If the conversation doesn't go well the first time

It's common for this not to land perfectly on the first attempt, especially if it comes right after a stressful incident. Leaving it a few days and returning to it more calmly — ideally alongside a concrete news story or a call you've personally received — usually works better than pushing the point in the moment.
