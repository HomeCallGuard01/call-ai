How to Protect Elderly Parents from Phone Scams

A practical, UK-focused overview of everything that actually helps — from spotting the warning signs to the specific tools and services worth setting up.

Phone scams targeting older people are common in the UK, and the callers behind them are often organised and convincing. Around four in five UK adults report receiving a suspected scam call or text in a recent three-month period, and people aged 75 and over are disproportionately targeted by callers impersonating banks, police, and government departments. This guide brings together the things that genuinely reduce the risk, in one place.

Start with the warning signs, not the technology

Before any call-blocking tool or service, the single most protective thing is your parent recognising the common patterns scammers use. Most scam calls share some combination of:

  • Urgency — you must act now, before you've had time to think or check with anyone.
  • Secrecy — you're told not to tell your bank, your family, or the police what's happening.
  • An unexpected problem — your account has been compromised, a payment has failed, your computer has a virus.
  • A request that moves money or data — transferring funds to a "safe account", reading out a one-time passcode, or handing a card to a courier.

See our full guide to identifying a scam phone call for the specific phrases and tactics to listen for.

Agree on a simple family rule in advance

The most effective single piece of advice: agree, ahead of time, that your parent will never act on a phone call alone — no bank transfers, no card details, no gift cards, no letting anyone into the house — without hanging up and calling a trusted family member or the organisation directly, using a number they already have written down (not one given by the caller).

This is easier to agree calmly in advance than to explain in the middle of a stressful call. See talking to an elderly parent about scam calls for how to raise this without it feeling like an accusation.

Reduce the number of scam calls that get through

Several UK tools genuinely cut down how many calls reach the phone in the first place:

  • The Telephone Preference Service (TPS) — the UK's free "do not call" register for marketing calls. Registering is free (0345 070 0707 or online) and legitimate companies are legally required to check against it. It's worth doing, but it's important to know its limits: it does not stop scam calls, since scammers don't follow the law that requires checking the register.
  • Call-blocking devices and services — purpose-built landline call blockers, provider-level nuisance call features, and monitoring services like Home Call Guard can screen or intercept far more than TPS alone. See our landline-specific guide.
  • Asking their phone provider about call-blocking features — BT, Sky, Virgin Media and others all offer some form of nuisance-call blocking, often free, sometimes requiring a small setup step.

Know where to report it

If your parent receives a suspicious call but hasn't lost money, it's worth reporting the number for free by texting "Call" followed by the number to 7726. If money has been lost, contact the bank immediately and report it to Action Fraud on 0300 123 2040 or at actionfraud.police.uk. Full detail in our reporting guide.

If a call claims to be from a bank, right now

Hang up and dial 159 — the UK's bank fraud hotline. It routes directly to the caller's own bank's fraud team, and unlike a normal phone number, 159 cannot be spoofed by a scammer. It works with over 99% of UK retail bank accounts.

If it's already happening regularly

If your parent is already getting scam calls often, a calm, practical response plan matters more than any single tool. See what to do if an elderly parent keeps getting scam calls for the exact steps.
