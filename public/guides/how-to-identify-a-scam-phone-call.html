Understanding scams

How to Identify a Scam Phone Call

Scam callers rely on a fairly small set of tactics, repeated endlessly. Once you know them, they're much easier to spot.

Phone scammers are often well-rehearsed and can sound entirely professional and convincing — this is by design. But most scam calls share the same underlying pressure tactics, regardless of who they're pretending to be.

The tactics almost every scam call uses

Urgency

"This needs to be sorted right now" or "you only have a few minutes before your account is locked." Genuine banks, HMRC, and the police do not require immediate, same-call action on financial matters. Urgency is a pressure tool to stop you thinking clearly or checking with anyone else.

Secrecy

Being told not to tell your bank, your family, or anyone else what's happening — sometimes framed as "this is part of the investigation" or "the fraudster may have access to your line." No genuine organisation will ever ask you to keep their call secret from your own family or bank.

An unexpected, alarming problem

"We've detected fraud on your account", "your computer has a virus", "there's a warrant out for your arrest", "your National Insurance number has been used in a crime." These are designed to produce a strong emotional reaction that overrides careful thinking.

A request that moves money or data

The call almost always ends with a specific ask: transfer money to a "safe account", read out a one-time passcode sent by text, hand your card to a courier, buy gift cards, or grant remote access to a computer. This is the actual goal of the call — everything before it is just building pressure toward this moment.

How genuine organisations actually behave

  • Banks will never ask you to move money to a "safe account" — there is no such thing. This exact phrase is one of the most reliable signs of a scam.
  • Banks, HMRC and the police will never ask you to read out a one-time passcode or PIN over the phone.
  • The police will never ask you to withdraw cash or hand your card to a courier to "assist an investigation."
  • None of them will object to you hanging up and calling them back on a number you look up yourself.

The single most reliable test

Hang up and call back independently

Whatever the call claims, hang up and call the organisation back using a number you already have — from a bank card, a bill, or the official website — never a number the caller gives you, and ideally from a different phone if possible (some scammers stay on the line pretending the call has ended). If it was genuine, calling back this way causes no harm at all.

Don't rely on caller ID alone

A call showing a real bank or government number on the screen is not proof it's genuine — this can be faked. See our guide to caller ID and number spoofing for why.
