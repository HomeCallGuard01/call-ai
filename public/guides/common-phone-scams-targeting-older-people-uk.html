Common Phone Scams Targeting Older People in the UK

A reference guide to the scams that come up most often — so you know what you're looking at as soon as it starts.

UK Finance data suggests around 1 in 6 scam cases now start with a phone call or text, and older people — particularly those aged 75 and over — are disproportionately targeted by callers impersonating trusted organisations. Here are the ones that come up most often.

Courier fraud

A caller claims to be from your bank, the police, or a fraud investigation unit, saying your card or account is compromised. They ask you to withdraw cash, or hand over your bank card and PIN, which a "courier" — sometimes a taxi driver, sometimes someone posing as a police officer — then collects in person. City of London Police data shows courier fraud losses exceeded £21 million in a recent 12-month period, with people over 70 disproportionately targeted.

The tell: no genuine bank or police investigation ever needs you to hand over a physical card or cash to a courier.

Tech support scams

A caller (or a pop-up on a computer with a phone number to call) claims your device has a virus or has been hacked, and offers to fix it — usually by asking for remote access to your computer, or payment for unnecessary "support." These scams specifically target older adults, on the assumption they may be less familiar with how legitimate tech support actually works.

The tell: Microsoft, Apple, and other legitimate tech companies do not make unsolicited calls about a virus on your device.

Pension scams

Fraudsters offer a "free pension review", guaranteed high returns, early access to pension savings before age 55, or unusual overseas investment opportunities. Once money is transferred out of a legitimate, regulated pension scheme into one of these arrangements, it's often impossible to recover. The FCA and Age UK both warn that any offer to release pension cash before 55 should be treated as almost certainly a scam.

Bank impersonation ("safe account" scams)

A caller claims to be from your bank's fraud team, says suspicious activity has been detected, and asks you to move your money to a "safe account" to protect it — or asks you to read out a one-time passcode that's just arrived by text. There is no such thing as a bank-provided "safe account", and no bank ever needs a one-time passcode read aloud over the phone.

Family emergency ("grandparent") scams

A caller claims to be a grandchild or other relative in urgent trouble — needing bail money, an emergency payment, or help with a sudden crisis — and asks for money to be sent quickly, often insisting the parents "mustn't be told." Advances in AI voice-cloning have made these calls sound more convincing in recent years, sometimes mimicking a real family member's voice.

The tell: hang up and call the family member directly on their known number to check, every time — regardless of how convincing the voice sounded.
