What to Do If an Elderly Parent Keeps Getting Scam Calls

If it's already a regular problem, here's a calm, practical order to work through it in.

Finding out a parent is getting scam calls repeatedly is unsettling, but it's a common and solvable problem. Here's a sensible order to work through it.

1. Find out if any money or information has already been given

Gently and without judgement, ask whether they've given out any details, transferred money, or let anyone into the house as a result of a call. If they have:

If money or details have already been shared

  • Contact their bank immediately — every UK bank has a fraud team, reachable via 159, the UK's bank fraud hotline, which cannot be spoofed and connects to the right bank automatically.
  • Report it to Action Fraud at 0300 123 2040 or actionfraud.police.uk (Police Scotland on 101 if in Scotland) — you'll get a crime reference number the bank may need.
  • If a card or cash was handed to someone in person (courier fraud), this is also a police matter, not just a bank one.

Acting quickly genuinely improves the chances of stopping or reversing a transfer, so this step comes first, before anything else on this page.

2. Report the number, even with no loss

If a call was suspicious but nothing was given away, report the number for free by texting "Call" followed by the number to 7726. This helps providers block it for others too.

3. Reduce how many calls get through going forward

Once the immediate situation is handled, put layers in place so it happens less:

  • Register their number with the Telephone Preference Service (free).
  • Ask their landline or mobile provider what call-blocking features they offer and switch them on.
  • Consider a monitoring service that watches for scam patterns in calls that get through regardless of number-blocking — see our full guide to stopping scam calls to elderly parents.

4. Agree a simple rule together, calmly

Once things are calmer, agree together: no money, card details, passwords, or one-time codes are ever given over the phone, and no one is let into the house, without hanging up first and calling a trusted family member or the organisation directly using a number you already have.

This lands much better as a shared plan than as a rule imposed after the fact — see talking to an elderly parent about scam calls.

5. Keep an eye on the pattern

If calls continue frequently despite the steps above, it may be worth checking bank statements together periodically for anything unfamiliar, and keeping a simple note of when calls happen — this can help identify whether a particular scam campaign is targeting the number specifically.
