Caller ID and Number Spoofing Explained

Why the number on your screen isn't proof of who's actually calling — and what's being done about it.

One of the most common questions after a scam call is "but it showed my bank's real number — how?" The answer is a technique called number spoofing, and understanding it changes how much you can trust caller ID on its own.

What is number spoofing?

Caller ID works by trusting a piece of information the calling system sends along with the call, called the "presentation number." Number spoofing is when a caller deliberately sets this to a different number than the one they're actually calling from — often a real bank, government department, or well-known company's genuine number — so the call looks legitimate before you've even answered.

Fraudsters based overseas particularly rely on this, since people are far more likely to answer a call that appears to come from a familiar UK number than an unknown international one.

What's being done about it

Ofcom, the UK's communications regulator, has required phone providers to tackle this directly. Since guidance came into force in January 2025, UK providers must identify and block calls arriving from abroad that spoof a UK landline number, except in a small number of legitimate business cases. Some providers reported blocking around a million such calls a day within the first month of enforcement.

Ofcom has also worked with major UK mobile operators — including BT/EE, Vodafone, Virgin Media O2, Three, TalkTalk, Tesco Mobile and Sky — on a wider charter to strengthen network-level anti-spoofing measures, and has proposed further restrictions on caller ID for UK mobile numbers roaming abroad where their validity can't be verified.

What this means for you in practice

  • A call showing a recognised bank or government number is not proof the call is genuine, even though network-level protections are improving.
  • The safest response to any call asking for money, personal details, or a passcode is to hang up and call back on a number you already have — from a bank card, a bill, or the official website — never a number given during the call.
  • The 159 banking fraud hotline exists specifically because it can't be spoofed — dialling it always reaches your real bank's fraud team, regardless of what any previous call displayed.

Why 159 is different

Ordinary phone numbers can be spoofed by a caller; 159 is routed differently and cannot be. That's why it's treated as the reliable way to check whether a call claiming to be your bank was genuine, rather than calling any number the original caller provided.
